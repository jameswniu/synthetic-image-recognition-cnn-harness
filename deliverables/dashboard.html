Checkbox reading, what it did and where it struggled

Every number on this page came out of an actual run over 61 pages and 5,872 checkboxes. None of it is a mock-up or a worked example; this is what the software did. Run make dashboard yourself and this exact page comes back, byte for byte; make synth && make telemetry re-measures the damaged corpus from scratch, and scripts/fetch_holdout.py adds the five real appraisals.

What is in this run. The brief supplied 4 pages, 287 checkboxes. I made 52 more by drawing marks into the two blank federal forms and damaging them on purpose, and fetched 5 real appraisals it had never seen. That is 61 pages and 5,872 checkboxes in total, and every count below names which of those three groups it came from.

A checkbox that only one of the two reads sees is either a form that changed or a box that was missed, and no single read can tell those apart. The two reads settle only where the boxes are, not whether they are marked: one finds them by their printed borders on this page, the other projects them from the blank original of that form.

A loan file never waits on us, and no page gets a silent guess. On 31 of 61 pages we double-checked every box against the blank original form; on the other 30, mostly scans I damaged, the page was too rough to line up with the original, so we read it once and labeled it read once, not double-checked. A page like that is usually just a bad scan, but it could be a newer form than the one we hold, or a page someone changed. We pass it anyway, in good faith, and the flag points the customer, ahead of time, at exactly the pages that could be an out-of-version form or fraud.

Who is the apprentice?

A small neural network trained from scratch on my own crops, 23,381 weights in a 29KB file, the only model in the system, and nothing it sees leaves the building. The LLM that built this product wrote both contestants, the rules and this model, and judged them against one referee: 52 damaged pages plus the human-ruled answer keys. The 286 graded boxes below are the 287 detections on the brief pages minus the one sitting on the tick the labeler ruled unsure, which no key can grade.

Verdict: rules only. All three are nearly errorless on the brief's answer key; they differ in how much they hand to people, 2 against 19 against 56. Most automation at the same accuracy wins. All three run on our own CPU, and the CNN adds 3.9 ms to a 118-box page (make bench-cnn). Nothing leaves the building.

The rules won the match, so the CNN stays switched off.

  • It caught nothing the rules missed, and on the two hard boxes on the photographed page it settled neither: it queued the empty pen loop at p(filled) 0.845 and read the filled faded X as empty at 0.082.
  • Switched on, it only adds work: 17 more boxes to people, zero errors caught.
  • Legibility is the invariant. The rules have it built in, every answer carries a reason a person can check, nothing extra needed. The CNN can only approach it by emitting logs, a confidence and a heat map for every box it reads, replayable forever.

The one dial, and what it costs to move

Each dot is one checkbox, laid left to right by how much ink is inside it, and grouped by what a person ruled it. The dashed line is where the shipped policy starts calling a box marked. 49 of the 76 crops have an ink measurement; the rest were ruled on shape alone and are not plotted.

How to read it.
  • The empty-lane dot right of the line is a pen loop crossing an empty box. There is real ink in it, so the ink test alone says marked. A second test asks whether the ink sits inside the box or runs straight through it, catches this one, and sends it to a person.
  • Move the line right and you miss faint real marks. Move it left and you call more empty boxes marked. Neither setting fixes a dot like that one, so where the line sits is the customer's call, in policy.json.
  • Unsure means the person labelling could not call it either, a circle in the box, a half-erased tick. Those are excluded from grading, and the system is required to send them to a person rather than decide; being confidently right on one would count against it as much as being wrong.

Seven versions of work, and none of it moved the score. It went into trusting the right things.
  • v1 graded itself against an answer key it had written, a meaningless 1.0. A person corrected the key at v2; the honest record starts there.
  • v4 added the second read from the blank original and the queue went UP, 52 to 61. It surfaces disagreements the first read cannot see about itself; the versions after resolve them.
  • v5 collapsed the queue, 61 to 4, by setting the bar where real marks sit. The old rule wanted a stroke across 70% of the box, which no clean X reaches, so real marks were flagged unsure by construction.
  • v6 caught a fake box, 4 to 2. The software had read the printed word "Att." as a checkbox, and the answer key, copied from the software's own output, agreed. The blank form says that row holds three boxes, not four; the key was fixed by hand, and a wrong-size box with letters inside is now rejected.
  • v7 moved the definition of a mark out of the code into policy.json, the settings file the customer owns. A circle, a strike-out, a thin stroke, the ink thresholds: changing them is now their edit, not our release. Nothing moved that day, and a gate asserts the shipped file still reproduces these numbers.

What this looks like in the production pipeline

99% right sounds fine for one checkbox, but a page compounds it. At 96 boxes a page, a 99% reader gets an entirely clean page only 0.9996 = 38% of the time, so 62% of pages carry at least one wrong answer, and nobody knows which pages. The gate is what changes that: every box the system cannot settle goes to a person instead of into the file, so the leftover is not a hidden error rate, it is a review pile you can see and staff.

  • So legibility is the invariant. When you cannot know which pages are wrong, the only defence is that every answer carries its reason and its evidence, so any one of them can be pulled and checked. That never gets traded away.
  • And automation is the optimization. Within that constraint, push the share that needs no person as high as it will go.
  • Next: turn the review pile into a feedback loop. Today a flag says box 12, page 3, and a human ruling ends there. With every box carrying its official field name, the same ruling keeps working:
    1. A flagged box arrives named, say Flood zone, instead of box 12, page 3.
    2. A reviewer rules it once.
    3. The ruling flows back two ways: as a label the automation trains on, and into counts like "the flood-zone question is mismarked on one appraisal in fifty," which the customer fixes by rewording the form.
    4. Both ends shrink tomorrow's pile, so the same hour of review clears today and buys automation for tomorrow, the steepest push up that curve available.
    The missing piece is the semantic layer. The blank forms we hold are positional data only, where each box sits; they do not yet carry what each box asks. Adding that layer of meaning is roadmap item 2 and is not built here.
  • And the expertise grows, but on a leash, because this is finance. The system does become the expert, the way a junior underwriter does: one signed-off case type at a time. Reviewers rule fifty circled boxes, the circles keep coming out marked, and it earns the right to settle circles alone on those forms, as one line in the policy file the customer holds. What it can never do is quietly start deciding a kind of box nobody ruled on.
